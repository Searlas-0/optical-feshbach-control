\begin{abstract}
    Optical Feshbach resonances couple an ultracold entrance channel to a decaying molecular state, so laser intensity and detuning jointly control both dispersive interactions and inelastic conversion. We formulate this as a bounded optimal-control problem: the laser determines a complex scattering length, a half-order Volterra equation propagates the history-dependent pair amplitude, and the loss--contact relation defines a dimensionless accumulated molecular objective. Automatic differentiation through this nonlocal dynamics permits multi-start optimisation of the complete intensity and detuning histories. A completed $N=100$ campaign contains \ShowcaseSweepRuns{} runs over \ShowcaseSweepCaps{} intensity caps, with \ShowcaseStartsPerCap{} starts per cap; the showcase displays every seed at every cap. Later searches give a stable saved value near $\Jmol=\ShowcaseFortyObjective$ at cap 40 and a time-limited selected endpoint $\Jmol=\ShowcaseThreeTwentyObjective$ at cap 320. At cap 1280, however, the objective continues to grow through increasingly grid-scale controls. The current calculation therefore establishes a power- and methodology-sensitive numerical landscape, not a converged high-power optimum or absolute molecule yield. Kernel normalisation, initialisation, time-grid convergence, the reduced optical map, and the experimental counting prefactor remain explicit validation requirements.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{optical Feshbach resonance, ultracold quantum gas, Tan contact, molecular formation, optimal control, Volterra equation, JAX}

    \showcaseheading{Physical control problem}
    {\sffamily\fontsize{9.4}{11.7}\selectfont
    An optical Feshbach resonance is a laser-induced resonance between two colliding atoms in an open scattering channel and an optically excited molecular bound state in a closed channel. In the photon-dressed energy picture, the molecule and an absorbed laser photon are treated as one combined state. Changing the laser frequency then moves its effective bound-state energy $E_c$ relative to the open-channel scattering energy $E$. Strong resonance requires $E_c-E\approx0$; for ultralow threshold collisions $E\approx0$, so resonance requires $E_c\approx0$.

    \vspace{1.5mm}
    Once the channels are near resonance, detuning $\nu$ controls the remaining energy mismatch, while laser intensity sets the channel coupling $W(I)$ and stimulated width $\Gamma\propto|W|^2$. Because the excited molecule decays at rate $\gamma$, the resonance produces a \emph{complex} scattering length: the dispersive interaction and inelastic molecular flux are linked responses, not independent controls.

    \vspace{1.5mm}
    The conversion rate is also nonlocal in time. A short-range pair amplitude $\eta(t)$ retains the complete earlier scattering history, so optimising a constant laser setting is not equivalent to optimising a pulse.}

    \vspace{2mm}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[showcasecard,colframe=showcasered!65,colback=showcasered!5,top=1.5mm,bottom=1.5mm]
        \showcasesubheading{Coupling--loss constraint}
        {\sffamily\fontsize{8.6}{10.7}\selectfont
        Near resonance, the closed-channel fraction scales approximately as
        \[
            |b|^2\propto
            \frac{|W(I)|^2}{(E-E_c)^2+(\hbar\gamma/2)^2}.
        \]
        Detuning suppresses both admixture and decay; intensity strengthens both. The useful operating path must therefore be found dynamically.}
    \end{tcolorbox}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
%==========================================================

    \rhostart{F}eshbach resonances make the interaction strength of an ultracold gas a controllable experimental parameter. At low collision energy, this control is summarised by the $s$-wave scattering length, and resonant coupling can change both its magnitude and sign \cite{chin2010feshbach}. Optical coupling adds speed and spatial selectivity, including for species without useful magnetic resonances, at the cost of spontaneous-emission and photoassociation loss \cite{bohn1997prospects,enomoto2008optical}.

    The central difficulty is dynamical. Molecular production at one instant depends not only on the laser settings at that instant, but also on the short-range pair correlations accumulated over the entire preceding protocol. Tan's contact organises these correlations \cite{tan2008energetics,braaten2008exact}, while the universal short-time theory of Qi, Shi, and Zhai reduces their evolution to a history-dependent equation for a pair amplitude $\eta(t)$ \cite{qi2021maximum}. This combination turns molecular formation into an optimal-control problem with memory.

    \begin{rhoenv}[frametitle=Central research question]
        For a fixed observation time $T$ and a maximum available laser coupling $\Gamma_{\max}$, which experimentally realisable intensity and detuning trajectories maximise the accumulated molecular yield, and when does increasing $\Gamma_{\max}$ cease to improve that yield?
    \end{rhoenv}

    \subsection{Motivation and scope}

        Abstract control of the real and imaginary parts of a complex scattering length can generate protocols that no laser can realise. This work instead uses the physical laser variables $\Gamma(t)$ and $\nu(t)$ and enforces $0\leq\Gamma(t)\leq\Gamma_{\max}$. A smoothness penalty and a posteriori slew-rate tests distinguish numerically optimal pulses from controls that can plausibly be produced in the laboratory.

        The intended output is a cap-response curve: optimised yield as a function of $\Gamma_{\max}/\gamma$, spanning the tight-cap and saturation regimes. The physical representative is fixed to bosonic $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$, using the $^1S_0$--$^3P_1$ intercombination line near $689\,\mathrm{nm}$ and an excited $\mathrm{Sr}_2$ photoassociation level. This choice supplies measured linewidth, optical-length, cloud, and laser scales for translating the dimensionless calculation \cite{blatt2011measurement,yan2013controlling}.

    \subsection{Objectives and contributions}

        This report is organised around the following deliverables:

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item verify the optical two-channel mapping and its limiting cases;
            \item validate a discrete solver for the half-order Volterra dynamics;
            \item optimise bounded physical controls with a reproducible multi-start procedure;
            \item calibrate the learning-rate schedule and control smoothness, then test convergence, grid dependence, and seed sensitivity;
            \item identify cap-binding and cap-insensitive regimes in the molecular yield; and
            \item translate one optimised dimensionless protocol into experimentally meaningful intensity, detuning, and duration.
        \end{enumerate}

        The present work completes the first cap-response campaign and two generations of regularisation search. The tight-cap result is reproducible across several optimiser stages, while the intermediate-cap value improves under targeted refinement. The principal new finding is negative but consequential: at high cap, longer optimisation increases $\Jmol$ by creating structure on the $N=100$ time scale, so the apparent upper envelope is not grid validated. The study therefore narrows the trustworthy operating region and specifies the resolution tests needed before an optical-power optimum can be claimed.

\section{Physical Background}\label{sec:background}
%==========================================================

    \subsection{Two-channel picture}

        An optical Feshbach resonance is a laser-induced resonance between an open atomic scattering channel and an optically excited molecular bound state in a closed channel. The two-body state is consequently decomposed as
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:two-channel-state}
            \lvert\Psi\rangle
            =\lvert\psi_o\rangle\lvert o\rangle
            +\lvert\psi_c\rangle\lvert c\rangle.
        \end{equation}

        When a laser couples these channels with strength $W$, the states mix. Near an isolated closed-channel energy level $E_c$, write $\lvert\psi_c\rangle\simeq b\lvert\phi_c\rangle$. Projecting the stationary Schrödinger equation onto this level gives the closed-channel amplitude
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-channel-amplitude}
            b=\frac{\langle\phi_c\rvert W^\dagger\lvert\psi_o\rangle}
            {E-E_c+\ii\hbar\gamma/2}
            ,
        \end{equation}
        where $\gamma$ is the decay rate of the excited molecule. The closed-channel admixture, which scales as $P_c\propto\lvert b\rvert^2$, is therefore enhanced near resonance but suppressed by large energy detuning or weak coupling. This mechanism provides the microscopic origin of the resonant, lossy control used below \cite{chin2010feshbach}.

    \subsection{Low-energy scattering}

        The open-channel dynamics at low energy are governed by the $s$-wave scattering length $a_s$. Outside the interaction range, the zero-energy scattering wavefunction takes the asymptotic form
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:scattering-length-boundary}
            \psi_o(r)\propto1-\frac{a_s}{r}.
        \end{equation}

        Purely elastic scattering yields a real scattering length. The molecular decay $\gamma$ introduced in the two-channel picture instead removes flux irreversibly, requiring a complex scattering length. With the time-dependence and $+\ii\gamma/2$ denominator convention used by the implementation,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:complex-scattering-convention}
            a_s=\alpha-\ii\beta,
            \qquad \beta\geq0.
        \end{equation}
        The real part $\alpha$ controls the elastic phase shift, while $\beta$ captures irreversible flux into molecular or radiative channels \cite{bohn1997prospects}. Consequently $\operatorname{Im}(1/a_s)\geq0$ wherever $a_s\ne0$. We employ a zero-range model that enforces this boundary condition while neglecting finite-range microscopic structure inside the interaction region.

    \subsection{Short-time pair amplitude}

        Consider two identical atoms of mass $m$ in their relative coordinate $\mathbf r$. At times short compared with the many-body timescale, their $s$-wave dynamics are governed by the zero-range Schrödinger equation with the Fermi--Huang pseudopotential:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:time-dependent-pseudopotential}
            \begin{aligned}
                \ii\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
                =\Bigg[&-\frac{\hbar^2}{m}\nabla^2\\
                &+\frac{4\pi\hbar^2}{m}a_s(t)\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)
                \frac{\partial}{\partial r}r\Bigg]\psi.
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}

        We assume an initially uniform, noninteracting state,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:noninteracting-initial-state}
            \psi(\mathbf r,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal V}},
            \qquad a_s(0)=0,
        \end{equation}
        where $\mathcal V$ is the normalisation volume. Thus the initial position-space probability density is uniform, $\lvert\psi(\mathbf r,0)\rvert^2=1/\mathcal V$.

        To solve the short-time dynamics, absorb the time-dependent contact boundary condition into the length-valued short-range amplitude
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:eta-definition}
            \eta(t)=-4\pi a_s(t)\sqrt{\mathcal V}
            \lim_{r\to0}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}[r\psi(r,t)].
        \end{equation}
        This definition turns the interaction into a time-dependent point source at the origin. Applying Duhamel's formula with the free-particle Green function separates the solution into a $1/r$ spatial singularity and a regular, history-dependent remainder. This finite history is governed by the half-order operator \cite{qi2021maximum}
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:half-order-operator}
            \begin{aligned}
                \mathcal L_{1/2}\eta(t)
                &=c\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\Bigg[
                \int_0^{t-\epsilon}
                \frac{\eta(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{3/2}}\,\dd\tau\\
                &\hspace{23mm}
                -\frac{2\eta(t)}{\sqrt\epsilon}
                \Bigg],
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}
        where
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:history-prefactor}
            c=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\hbar}}\,
            \frac{1}{8\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{\ii}}.
        \end{equation}

        Evaluating the spatial derivative of the Green-function solution near the origin recovers the boundary condition and closes the dynamics as a single Volterra integral equation for $\eta(t)$:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:volterra}
            \left[\mathcal L_{1/2}
            +\frac{1}{4\pi a_s(t)}\right]\eta(t)=-1.
        \end{equation}

        Once $\eta(t)$ is determined, it fixes the short-distance form of the wavefunction through the Bethe--Peierls boundary condition
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:bethe-peierls-eta}
            \psi(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{\eta(t)}{4\pi\sqrt{\mathcal V}}
            \left[\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{a_s(t)}\right]+\mathcal O(r).
        \end{equation}
        Because the system starts noninteracting, $\eta(0)=0$, and its subsequent evolution encodes the full history of the controlled scattering length.

    \subsection{Tan's contact and universal short-time dynamics}

        Tan's contact $\mathcal{C}$ quantifies short-range pair correlations and appears, for example, in the large-momentum tail
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:contact-tail}
            n_{\sigma}(k)\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}}{k^4}.
        \end{equation}
        It connects microscopic pair structure to thermodynamic and dynamical observables \cite{tan2008energetics,braaten2008exact}. For a gas initially in a noninteracting state, the universal short-time reduction used here writes
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:contact-eta}
            \mathcal{C}(t)=g_2(0)\,|\eta(t)|^2,
        \end{equation}
        where $g_2(0)$ is the initial local pair density and $\eta(t)$ is determined by two-body short-distance physics \cite{qi2021maximum}.

        A real square-root ramp, $a_s(t)\propto\sqrt{\hbar t/m}$, gives the scale-invariant onset studied in Ref.~\cite{qi2021maximum}. It provides both a conceptual benchmark and a numerical limiting case for the present extension to complex $a_s$.

    \subsection{Laser control of the scattering length}

        Up to this point, $a_s(t)$ is an arbitrary prescribed interaction
        history. For the optical-control problem, Eq.~\eqref{eq:volterra} is
        analytically continued to a complex scattering length and that history
        is restricted to values accessible through laser intensity and
        detuning. A ``photon-dressed'' closed channel is the coupled
        molecule--field state after accounting for the absorbed photon;
        changing the laser frequency shifts its effective energy relative to
        the open-channel threshold.

        It is useful to separate the dimensional one-pole result from the
        additional assumptions made by this project.  Eliminating one decaying
        molecular state in the renormalised zero-range model of Liu, Shi, and
        Wang gives \cite{liu2024weakly}
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:ofr-one-pole}
            \ac(E,I)=\abg\left[
                1+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)}
                {E-\nu_{\rm re}(I)+\ii\gamma/2}
            \right].
        \end{equation}
        Here $\Gamma_{\rm s}$ is a \emph{signed} resonant-strength parameter,
        while $\nu_{\rm re}$ is the coupling-renormalised molecular energy.
        Equation~\eqref{eq:ofr-one-pole} is already an isolated-resonance,
        zero-range description, but it does not by itself require the light
        shift $\nu_{\rm re}-\nu$ to equal $\Gamma_{\rm s}$.

        The outline makes two further choices: threshold scattering, $E=0$,
        and the parameterisation
        $\nu_{\rm re}(I)=\nu+\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)$.  Microscopically,
        $\Gamma_{\rm s}=m|\alpha_{\rm re}|^2/(4\pi\abg)$ and therefore has
        the sign of $\abg$.  The implementation instead stores the ordinary
        non-negative magnitude $\Gamma=|\Gamma_{\rm s}|$ and carries
        $s=\operatorname{sgn}(\abg)$ explicitly, so that
        $\Gamma_{\rm s}=s\Gamma$.  Its dimensional map is consequently
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:ofr-dimensional}
            \ac(I,\nu)=\abg\left[
                1+\frac{s\Gamma(I)}{-s\Gamma(I)-\nu+\ii\gamma/2}
            \right].
        \end{equation}
        For a positive background this reduces directly to the outline's
        formula. For $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$, $s=-1$: the code can retain the
        convenient bound $\Gamma\geq0$ without changing loss into gain. Here
        $\Gamma(I)\propto|W|^2\propto I$ is the weak-field stimulated-width
        magnitude, $\nu$ is the model detuning, and $\gamma$ is the molecular
        decay rate.  All four quantities in the denominator must be expressed
        consistently as energies, angular frequencies, or ordinary
        frequencies; factors of $2\pi$ cannot be mixed.  The real and imaginary
        parts of $\ac$ remain linked by the same two controls and cannot be
        optimised independently \cite{bohn1997prospects}. Appendix~\ref{app:ofr-map}
        gives the two-channel derivation, algebraic checks, sign translation,
        and assumption ledger.

        The identification
        $\nu_{\rm re}-\nu=\Gamma_{\rm s}$ is a simplifying control
        parameterisation, not a universal statement that an OFR light shift
        equals its resonance strength.  In $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$, the measured
        light-shift slopes even change sign between nearby $0_u$ levels, and an
        isolated-resonance expression fails once the detuning is comparable to
        the molecular-level spacing \cite{blatt2011measurement}.  A calibrated
        model should therefore allow separate strength and shift functions
        before the optimised waveform is interpreted quantitatively.

        Narrow intercombination transitions reduce the natural linewidth and can therefore produce appreciable scattering changes at moderate intensity. The early ytterbium demonstration changed the zero-energy scattering phase using this strategy \cite{enomoto2008optical}. More directly relevant here, optical Feshbach resonances have been measured in a thermal $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ gas and used to control the collapse and expansion of an $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ condensate \cite{blatt2011measurement,yan2013controlling}. The small negative background scattering length makes even changes of a few Bohr radii dynamically important.

\section{Model and Control Problem}\label{sec:model}
%==========================================================

    \subsection{Dimensionless variables}

        Choose a fixed positive reference time $t_\star$, independent of the
        as-yet unknown background scattering length. Its associated length is
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:reference-scales}
            t_\star=\frac{m\ell_\star^2}{\hbar},\qquad
            \ell_\star=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar t_\star}{m}},\qquad
            \tau=\frac{t}{t_\star},\qquad
            \widetilde a=\frac{a}{\ell_\star}.
        \end{equation}
        Optical frequencies are separately measured in units of $\gamma$:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:dimensionless-controls}
            u(\tau)=\frac{\Gamma(t)}{\gamma},\qquad
            v(\tau)=\frac{\nu(t)}{\gamma}.
        \end{equation}
        The background ratio and its derived sign are
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:background-ratio}
            r_{\rm bg}=\frac{\abg}{\ell_\star}
            =\sqrt{\frac{m}{\hbar t_\star}}\,\abg,
            \qquad s=\operatorname{sgn}(r_{\rm bg}).
        \end{equation}
        Equation~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensional} becomes the mapping implemented in the code,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:ofr-dimensionless}
            \widetilde a_c(t)
            =r_{\rm bg}\left[
                1+\frac{s u(t)}{-s u(t)-v(t)+\ii/2}
            \right].
        \end{equation}
        At $u=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensionless} reduces exactly to
        $\widetilde a_c=r_{\rm bg}$. The value $r_{\rm bg}=0$ is excluded
        because $s$ would be undefined. For the present $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$
        input, $r_{\rm bg}=-0.008716$ and hence the resonant denominator is
        $u-v+\ii/2$; the low- and intermediate-cap controls in Fig.~2(c)
        visibly track $v\simeq u$.
        The admissible controls obey
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:control-bounds}
            0\leq u(t)\leq u_{\max},\qquad
            |v(t)|\leq v_{\max},
        \end{equation}
        with $u_{\max}=\Gamma_{\max}/\gamma$.

        Once a fixed $t_\star$ is selected, each candidate mass and background
        length maps to a definite $r_{\rm bg}$ through
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:background-ratio}. This retains the physical dependence
        on $m$ and $\abg$ rather than absorbing it into a species-dependent
        ruler.

        With $t=t_\star\tau$, $\eta=\ell_\star\widetilde\eta$, and $t_\star=m\ell_\star^2/\hbar$, the dimensional factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:half-order-operator} cancels. The dimensionless history-kernel coefficient required by the short-time derivation is therefore
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:dimensionless-kernel-prefactor}
            \kappa_{\rm th}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{\ii}}.
        \end{equation}

        \begin{rhoenv}[frametitle=Unresolved solver convention]
            The continuous noninteracting derivation gives $\eta(0)=0$ and the
            coefficient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dimensionless-kernel-prefactor}. The
            current implementation instead initialises
            $\eta_0=-4\pi\widetilde a_c(0)$ and sets
            $\kappa_{\rm code}=-1/(4\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{\ii})$. These coefficients
            differ in both sign and a factor of two. This is a known model audit,
            not a small plotting uncertainty; all optimisation results remain
            provisional until an independent benchmark selects the correct
            convention.
        \end{rhoenv}

    \subsection{Molecular-yield objective}

        Under the convention in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensionless}, the loss--contact relation gives a non-negative product flux proportional to $\operatorname{Im}(1/\ac)|\eta|^2$. In the implemented dimensionless normalisation, the accumulated objective is
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:molecule-objective}
            \Jmol[u,v]
            =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^T
            \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\frac{1}{\ac(t)}\right)
            |\eta(t)|^2\,\dd t.
        \end{equation}
        This is the dimensionless, density-like quantity maximised in the current inelastic simulations. It is non-negative for either sign of $r_{\rm bg}$.

        \begin{rhoenv}[frametitle=Counting convention to resolve]
            Restoring the dimensional scales gives the density conversion
            $n_{\rm mol}=g_2(0)\ell_\star^3\Jmol$, because
            $g_2(0)$ has units $L^{-6}$. The remaining physical derivation must
            establish whether this normalisation counts depleted atoms, lost
            pairs, or product molecules, and which pair factor applies to the
            chosen gas. A positive global factor does not change the optimising
            protocol, but it changes every quoted density, fraction, and
            molecule number. Figure~\ref{fig:current-results} evaluates this
            conversion with the supplied $g_2(0)=1\,\mathrm{m}^{-6}$ and labels
            the result a \emph{density proxy}; it is not an experimentally
            calibrated absolute yield until the counting convention is fixed.
        \end{rhoenv}

    \subsection{Operational regularisation}

        Piecewise controls can exploit the discretisation by developing unrealistically sharp features. The current code penalises first and second differences of the \emph{physical dimensionless controls} $u$ and $v$ (not cap-normalised controls). With $\Delta t=T/N$, it subtracts
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothness}
            \begin{aligned}
            \Rsm={}&\frac{\lambda_{u,1}}{\Delta t}
                \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(u_{k+1}-u_k)^2
            +\frac{\lambda_{v,1}}{\Delta t}
                \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(v_{k+1}-v_k)^2\\
            &+\frac{\lambda_{u,2}}{\Delta t^3}
                \sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(u_{k+2}-2u_{k+1}+u_k)^2\\
            &+\frac{\lambda_{v,2}}{\Delta t^3}
                \sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(v_{k+2}-2v_{k+1}+v_k)^2.
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}
        The first-difference coefficients are configured through
        \texttt{smoothness}, and the second-difference coefficients through
        \texttt{sharpness}, with optional control-specific overrides. Because
        the amplitudes are not normalised by their caps, the same coefficient
        does not impose the same relative smoothing at different $u_{\max}$;
        this is one reason the coefficients were swept separately for each
        selected cap. The optimiser maximises the regularised score
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:score}
            \mathcal{S}=\Jmol-\Rsm,
        \end{equation}
        while all quoted molecular yields must report the unpenalised $\Jmol$ as well. A posteriori slew tests are applied separately to avoid interpreting the regulariser as a hardware model.

\section{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerics}
%==========================================================

    \subsection{Time discretisation and L1 history solver}

        The interval $[0,T]$ is divided into $N$ equal steps, $t_k=k\Delta t$, with controls represented at $N+1$ grid points. The L1 discretisation uses the history weights
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-weights}
            w_{k,j}=\sqrt{k-j}-\sqrt{k-j-1},
            \qquad 0\leq j\leq k-2.
        \end{equation}
        The implementation initialises
        $\eta_0=-4\pi\ac(t_0)$ and defines
        $q=2\kappa_{\rm code}/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with
        $\kappa_{\rm code}=-1/(4\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{\ii})$. For $k\geq1$, let
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-history}
            H_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}w_{k,j}(\eta_{j+1}-\eta_j),
        \end{equation}
        the implemented recurrence has the explicit form
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-update}
            \eta_k=
            \frac{-1+q(\eta_{k-1}-H_k)}
            {1/[4\pi\ac(t_k)]+q}.
        \end{equation}
        Molecular yield is evaluated with the composite trapezoidal rule. A
        direct scan of the $k$ earlier samples gives $O(N^2)$ arithmetic and
        $O(N)$ state storage for one control. Automatic differentiation has the
        same nonlocal dependency structure. The mismatch
        $\kappa_{\rm code}\ne\kappa_{\rm th}$ and the nonzero numerical
        initialisation are stated explicitly because optimiser stability cannot
        validate either choice.

    \subsection{Bounded physical controls}

        Unconstrained optimiser variables $x_k,y_k\in\mathbb{R}$ are mapped smoothly into the admissible laser domain:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:bounded-map}
            u_k=u_{\max}\,\operatorname{sigmoid}(x_k),\qquad
            v_k=v_{\max}\tanh(y_k).
        \end{equation}
        This parameterisation makes every iterate physical with respect to the amplitude bounds and remains differentiable for automatic differentiation. Saturation of either map can, however, reduce gradients; cap-usage diagnostics are therefore retained.

    \subsection{Gradient optimisation}

        The calculation follows the logic of GRAPE: discretise the controls, differentiate the terminal performance with respect to every time slice, and update the complete pulse \cite{khaneja2005optimal}. JAX differentiates through the scattering map, Volterra scan, quadrature, and regulariser. Batched Adam updates are compiled and vectorised, with 64-bit arithmetic used for production calculations.

        The learning rate is changed in stages without resetting Adam's moment estimates. Stability is evaluated at fixed checkpoint intervals independently of those stages. For run $r$, the reported solution is the best finite iterate according to $\mathcal{S}$ when smoothing is active and according to $\Jmol$ otherwise.

    \subsection{Multi-start strategy and convergence tests}

        Initialisation is campaign-specific and is archived per run. The completed nine-cap sweep uses 50 reproducible five-mode Fourier starts at every cap. Each of the first smoothness--sharpness grids contains $6\times5$ coefficient pairs and 15 starts per pair: ten new Fourier controls plus five controls selected from earlier cap-matched results, for 450 completed runs per cap. The later $15\times15$ exploratory grids use ten Fourier starts and one queried control per coefficient pair. Targeted peak and L-BFGS stages then resume only the strongest saved controls. This staged design allocates broad sampling to discovery and long trajectories to refinement, but it also means that best values from different stages are methodology-dependent upper envelopes rather than identically distributed estimates.

        The current stopping test is evaluated every $M$ optimiser steps in the normalised feasible controls, not in the unbounded sigmoid/$\tanh$ coordinates. With $\bar u=u/u_{\max}$ and $\bar v=v/v_{\max}$,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:physical-control-stability}
            \epsilon_u=\frac{\|\bar u_k-\bar u_{k-M}\|_2}{\sqrt{N+1}},\qquad
            \epsilon_v=\frac{\|\bar v_k-\bar v_{k-M}\|_2}{\sqrt{N+1}}.
        \end{equation}
        Score stability uses the relative change between successive block-mean scores. A run is accepted only after the score and every enabled control metric pass their tolerances for three consecutive checkpoints; peak refinement can additionally require a projected-gradient threshold in normalised control space. A saved best iterate is retained even when a wall-time limit or failure prevents this stopping rule from being met, which is why status is shown explicitly in Fig.~2.

        best values are $39.97$ and $81.676$; at $320$ they are
        $\ShowcaseBaselineThreeTwentyMedian$ and
        $\ShowcaseBaselineThreeTwentyBest$; and at $1280$ they are
        $\ShowcaseBaselineTwelveEightyMedian$ and
        $\ShowcaseBaselineTwelveEightyBest$, respectively. The separation

        physically vanishes above a threshold. Conversely, the large isolated
        best values at $u_{\max}=320$--$1280$ cannot be interpreted as a
        saturation curve. The completed sweep establishes strong cap and seed
        sensitivity, but it does not by itself locate a crossover or plateau.

    \subsection{Regularisation search and targeted refinement}

        Separate archived cap-40 and cap-1280 searches include completed
        $6\times5$ smoothness--sharpness grids. Cap 320 instead uses a dedicated
        crossover screen: 250 broad Fourier seeds, a monotone polish of every
        seed, and loose L-BFGS refinement of the top 20. The stages contain 520
        completed run records and expose strong seed dependence: their best
        objectives rise from $167.16$ in exploration to $436.73$ after
        all-seed polishing and $\ShowcaseThreeTwentyObjective$ in the top-20
        stage.

        The cap-40 value $\Jmol=\ShowcaseFortyObjective$ agrees to the displayed
        precision across long Adam, peak-refinement, and L-BFGS records. The
        selected cap-320 endpoint has
        $\Jmol=\ShowcaseThreeTwentyObjective$, but its L-BFGS run ended at the
        time limit after 1,250 iterations rather than satisfying the stability
        test. Moreover, the fixed-method cap-320 campaign contains a still
        larger rare-seed objective, $\ShowcaseBaselineThreeTwentyBest$. The
        selected point is therefore a source-labelled control for comparison,
        not a converged cap-320 optimum or an absolute molecule yield.

    \subsection{Control structure and the high-cap failure mode}

        Figure~\ref{fig:current-results}(c) now compares like with like: at each
        cap it takes the largest regularised-score run from the same completed
        fixed-method campaign and plots the archived $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ without
        dividing by $u_{\max}$ or $v_{\max}$. The tight group contains caps
        $10$--$160$, the crossover group contains $320$ and $640$, and the
        loose group contains $1280$ and $2560$. Separate vertical scales are
        used only between these three regimes, where the allowed amplitudes
        differ by orders of magnitude; within a regime the curves share one
        raw dimensionless scale and retain the cap colours used in panels (a)
        and (b). This presentation exposes the transition from predominantly
        cap-active tight controls to more structured crossover and loose-cap
        histories without conflating amplitude changes with normalisation.

        The separately targeted cap-1280 trajectory is not substituted into
        that fixed-method comparison. Its latest saved iterate reaches the
        density proxy
        $n_{\rm mol}=\ShowcaseTwelveEightyDensityScaled\times
        10^{-21}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}$ after
        \ShowcaseHighCapSteps{} steps, yet the objective is still increasing
        and none of the five strict cap-1280 members has passed the stopping
        rule. Its pulse contains narrow changes over one or a few grid
        intervals, including excursions near the endpoint. The regularisation
        penalty is finite, but it has not prevented the optimiser from using
        the $N=100$ discretisation as an additional degree of freedom. The
        increasing objective must therefore be treated as a lower bound on a
        \emph{discrete} optimisation trajectory, not as a physical optimum.
        Direct transfer of this fixed pulse from $N=100$ to 800 changes the
        reevaluated objective by $\ShowcaseTwelveEightyGridChangePercent\%$,
        and the change from $N=400$ to 800 is only
        $\ShowcaseTwelveEightyFineGridChangePercent\%$. This shows that the
        forward evaluation of the interpolated pulse is settling; it does not
        show that a fresh high-cap optimisation would recover the same narrow
        structure or objective.

    \subsection{What is and is not established}

        The data establish three points. First, finite intensity matters: the
        tight-cap protocol is cap-active and has a repeatedly recovered saved
        objective near $81.68$. Second, the optimisation landscape becomes
        strongly multimodal with increasing cap; population medians and best
        values answer different questions. Third, long high-cap searches expose
        grid-scale structure rather than a plateau. Accordingly, no crossover
        cap, saturation yield, low-cap scaling exponent, or experimentally
        calibrated molecule fraction is reported here.

        Figure~\ref{fig:current-results}(b) now separates one part of control
        optimisation from solver resolution. Between $N=100$ and 800, the
        fixed-control objective changes by $\ShowcaseFortyGridChangePercent\%$,
        $\ShowcaseThreeTwentyGridChangePercent\%$, and
        $\ShowcaseTwelveEightyGridChangePercent\%$ at caps 40, 320, and 1280,
        respectively. Independent reoptimisations must still test whether each
        pulse structure and objective persist under matched physical
        regularisation. Only after that test, the kernel/initialisation audit,
        and the counting-prefactor
        derivation should initialisation-seed and laser-calibration uncertainty
        be propagated into a physical yield.

    \subsection{Fixed representative: bosonic strontium-88}

        The physical reference is an $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ condensate in the
        $^1S_0+{}^1S_0$ entrance channel, optically coupled near the
        $^1S_0+{}^3P_1$ molecular asymptote. The atomic intercombination line is
        near $689\,\mathrm{nm}$; its measured $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ frequency is
        $434\,829\,121\,311(10)\,\mathrm{kHz}$
        \cite{ferrari2003precision}. The condensate experiment used the
        second-least-bound excited molecular level, with binding energy
        $h\times24\,\mathrm{MHz}$ below this asymptote
        \cite{yan2013controlling}.  The reduced-map baseline is
        $\abg=-1.4(6)a_0\simeq-7.41\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m}$; because this
        value is unusually close to zero, a sensitivity sweep over roughly
        $-2.0a_0$ to $-0.8a_0$ is more defensible than treating the central
        value as exact \cite{blatt2011measurement}.

        Two linewidths answer different questions.  The ideal radiative
        molecule has $\gamma_{\rm mol}=2\gamma_a$ with
        $\gamma_a/2\pi=7.5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, hence
        $\gamma_{\rm mol}/2\pi=15\,\mathrm{kHz}$.  The direct thermal-gas OFR
        measurement instead fitted an effective total width
        $\gamma_{\rm eff}/2\pi=40(5)\,\mathrm{kHz}$, incorporating additional
        broadening \cite{blatt2011measurement}.  The former is an ideal-theory
        input; the latter is the appropriate baseline for the present
        empirically broadened reference.  The figures use ordinary frequencies
        with $\gamma_f=40\,\mathrm{kHz}$ throughout.  An angular-frequency
        calculation is equivalent only if $\Gamma$, $\nu$, and $\gamma$ are all
        multiplied by $2\pi$.

        The measured intensity calibration is naturally expressed through the optical length $\ell_{\rm opt}$ rather than a universal constant $\xi$:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:sr-intensity-calibration}
            \ell_{\rm opt}=\beta I,\qquad
            \Gamma_{\rm stim}=2k_r\ell_{\rm opt}\Gamma_{\rm mol},\qquad
            \beta=2.2\times10^4\frac{a_0}{\mathrm{W\,cm^{-2}}},
        \end{equation}
        where $k_r$ is the relative collision wave number and
        $\Gamma_{\rm mol}=2\pi\times15\,\mathrm{kHz}$
        \cite{yan2013controlling}.  If the code's non-negative magnitude is
        identified as $\Gamma=\Gamma_{\rm stim}$, while
        $\gamma=\Gamma_{\rm mol}$ and $\nu=\Delta$, then
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:intensity-conversion}
            I_{\max}=\frac{u_{\max}}{2k_r\beta},\qquad
            \frac{\Delta(t)}{2\pi}=\left(15\,\mathrm{kHz}\right)v(t).
        \end{equation}
        Thus an intensity cap cannot be assigned until $k_r$ is fixed
        consistently from the simulated gas. By contrast, under this
        ideal-width choice the current $v_{\max}=1000$ maps directly to
        $|\Delta|/2\pi=15\,\mathrm{MHz}$; the same dimensionless bound would be
        $40\,\mathrm{MHz}$ under the effective-width choice used in the
        showcase dimensionalisation. Both exceed the $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$ range
        quoted for the reference condensate experiment and must not be mixed in
        one conversion.  More fundamentally,
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr-intensity-calibration} calibrates a positive
        stimulated linewidth, whereas the tied shift in
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensional} remains a model assumption; the two
        identifications require validation against the selected molecular
        level.

    \subsection{Experimentalist's recipe}

        Use $I=0.057\,\mathrm{W\,cm^{-2}}$, waist $725\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and
        detunings of order $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$ as a literature-anchored
        comparison point, not yet as the optimised recommendation. The quoted
        beam corresponds to approximately $0.47\,\mathrm{mW}$ if $I$ is the
        peak intensity of a Gaussian beam. Once the fixed reference time
        $t_\star$ has been selected, the reported $1.2$- and $4$-ms exposures
        must be converted using $\tau=t/t_\star$; they cannot be represented by
        silently setting the solver's $T=1$ equal to one millisecond.

        If the four reduced parameters $\abg$, $\Gamma(I)$, $\nu$, and
        $\gamma$ have been calibrated in one convention, no separate $C_6$ or
        van der Waals length is needed to evaluate the algebraic map.  Those
        microscopic quantities have already been compressed into its fitted
        inputs. They become necessary again when the isolated, zero-range
        reduction fails and a finite-range coupled-channel calculation is
        required. The minimum experimental data ledger is therefore the signed
        $\abg$, a level-specific strength calibration, an independently measured
        light shift, the appropriate total molecular width, and an unambiguous
        detuning origin.

        The experimental isolated-resonance model contains the fitted loss enhancement $\eta_{\rm loss}=19.5$ and a detuning-dependent background loss not present in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensional} \cite{yan2013controlling}. The subscript distinguishes this empirical loss parameter from the pair amplitude $\eta(t)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contact-eta}. The $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ prescription is therefore conditional on first verifying the mapping between the measured model and the implemented $\ac(u,v)$, especially the roles of $\Gamma_{\rm stim}$, $\Gamma_{\rm mol}$, and the phenomenological loss terms.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================

    \subsection{Physical interpretation}

        The low- and intermediate-cap controls suggest resonance tracking rather
        than a two-stage ``prepare then convert'' protocol. For
        $r_{\rm bg}<0$, increasing $u$ shifts the resonant denominator unless
        $v$ changes with it; the observed $v\simeq u$ relation compensates this
        shift while the intensity remains cap-active. The endpoint turn-off
        lowers both coupling and detuning together. Establishing a more detailed
        mechanism requires plotting $|\eta|^2$, instantaneous inelastic flux,
        and cumulative $\Jmol$ from a grid-converged pulse. In particular, the
        present data do not justify invoking contact preparation or a
        quantum-Zeno mechanism.

        The half-order memory dynamics are inherited from the universal
        short-time description of Ref.~\cite{qi2021maximum}, but the reachable
        interaction histories are not universal. The complex optical map,
        spontaneous linewidth, finite detuning range, cap-dependent
        regularisation, and bounded parameterisation introduce additional
        scales. Consequently, neither the real-scattering square-root ramp nor
        its scale-invariant growth law can be assumed to remain optimal in this
        lossy two-control problem; it must be evaluated as a common baseline
        after the solver convention is validated.

    \subsection{Limitations}

        The principal limitations affect different conclusions and should not be
        combined into one generic uncertainty bar.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Reduced optical map.} The calculation retains one
            $s$-wave entrance channel and one isolated, zero-range molecular
            state at $E=0$, with constant Markovian decay, weak-field
            $\Gamma\propto I$, a uniform laser, and no medium correction.  It
            additionally ties the light shift to the signed resonance strength,
            $\nu_{\rm re}-\nu=\Gamma_{\rm s}$.  Finite temperature, saturation,
            power broadening, spatial intensity variation, neighbouring
            $0_u/1_u$ levels, and independently calibrated AC Stark shifts are
            therefore absent; Appendix~\ref{app:ofr-map} records the benefit and
            failure mode of each assumption.
            \item \textbf{Kernel and initial condition.} The exact
            noninteracting theory has $\eta(0)=0$, whereas the recurrence starts
            from $-4\pi\ac(0)$ and uses a kernel coefficient that is not yet
            reconciled with Eq.~\eqref{eq:dimensionless-kernel-prefactor}. This
            can change both scale and pulse ranking. An analytic constant-$a_s$
            or square-root benchmark is required before production claims.
            \item \textbf{Finite time grid.} The high-cap optimiser develops
            one-interval features while $\Jmol$ continues to rise, which is
            consistent with upward discretisation bias. The fixed-control
            transfer in Fig.~\ref{fig:current-results}(b) is mildly
            grid-dependent and settles on its two finest grids, but matched
            reoptimisation across increasing $N$ remains the direct test of the
            optimised envelope.
            \item \textbf{Regularisation model.} Equation~\eqref{eq:smoothness}
            penalises absolute dimensionless controls, so its physical effect
            changes with the cap. A measured laser transfer function or
            bandwidth-constrained parameterisation would provide a defensible
            hardware model.
            \item \textbf{Local optimisation.} The widening best--median gap
            shows that rare basin discovery dominates the reported upper
            envelope. Larger seed populations improve discovery but cannot by
            themselves establish convergence to a global optimum.
            \item \textbf{Yield normalisation.} The missing counting prefactor
            does not change an optimiser under a positive global rescaling, but
            it prevents conversion of $\Jmol$ into a molecule fraction or
            number.
            \item \textbf{Many-body and optical physics.} Depletion, trap
            inhomogeneity, finite range, the measured
            $\eta_{\rm loss}=19.5$ enhancement, and far-wing background loss
            are omitted. These omissions generally make a laboratory yield less
            favourable than the idealised two-body objective.
            \item \textbf{Intensity calibration.} The conversion
            $u\mapsto I$ depends on collision momentum $k_r$ and therefore on
            the chosen cloud model. Until $k_r$ and $t_\star$ are fixed, the
            dimensionless pulse has no unique power or duration.
        \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Mathematical and computational extensions}

        The immediate computational extension is a grid ladder with identical
        source controls, not a more elaborate optimiser. Once a resolution-safe
        control basis is established, projected-gradient or L-BFGS polishing can
        replace millions of small Adam updates. A reduced Hessian spectrum can
        then quantify poorly constrained pulse directions, and robust control
        can optimise an ensemble over measured amplitude, detuning, and timing
        noise. These extensions become scientifically useful only after the
        forward Volterra solver has an independent benchmark.

%==========================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================

    A bounded optical Feshbach resonance produces a strongly cap-dependent
    control landscape because intensity changes both channel coupling and the
    resonance condition. At $N=100$, the cap-40 pulse is repeatedly recovered
    with a saved dimensionless objective near
    $\Jmol=\ShowcaseFortyObjective$, and targeted search reaches
    $\ShowcaseThreeTwentyObjective$ at cap 320, although that selected endpoint
    is time-limited. The cap-40 control tracks $v\simeq u$ within the assumed
    tied-shift map, whereas the cap-320 endpoint develops a distinct two-control
    history. At higher cap,
    population medians and rare best runs separate sharply, and the cap-1280
    objective continues to grow through grid-scale pulse structure. Direct
    forward reevaluation of the fixed selected controls changes modestly by
    $N=800$, but does not establish matched-grid optimisation convergence. The central
    answer is therefore conditional: finite optical power clearly shapes the
    reachable protocol, but the present data do not establish a crossover cap,
    a saturation yield, or an experimental molecule number. The next decisive
    steps are to reconcile the kernel and initialisation, reoptimise controls
    on the finer grids already used for fixed-pulse transfer, and only then restore the
    $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ density, collision-momentum, and counting prefactors.

\section{Origin, Checks, and Scope of the Optical Map}
\label{app:ofr-map}
%==========================================================

    This appendix makes explicit which parts of the optical map follow from a
    two-channel calculation and which parts are additional control-model
    assumptions.  Throughout the derivation only, set $\hbar=1$, so energies,
    detunings, linewidths, and rates have the same units.  Factors of $\hbar$
    are restored when dimensional experimental quantities are discussed.

    \subsection{Two-channel origin}

        Let $\hat b_{\mathbf k}$ annihilate a ground-state atom and
        $\hat d_{\mathbf q}$ an excited molecule.  The open-channel contact
        strength is $g$, the laser-induced atom--molecule coupling is $\alpha$,
        the bare molecular detuning is $\nu$, the molecular decay rate is
        $\gamma$, the atomic mass is $m$, and the quantisation volume is
        $\Omega$.  A minimal Hermitian Hamiltonian is
        \begin{align}
            \widehat H_{\rm OFR}={}&
            \sum_{\mathbf k}\epsilon_{\mathbf k}
                \hat b^\dagger_{\mathbf k}\hat b_{\mathbf k}
            +\sum_{\mathbf q}\left(\frac{q^2}{4m}+\nu\right)
                \hat d^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\hat d_{\mathbf q}\nonumber\\
            &+\frac{g}{2\Omega}
            \sum_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k',\mathbf p}
                \hat b^\dagger_{\mathbf k+\mathbf p}
                \hat b^\dagger_{\mathbf k'-\mathbf p}
                \hat b_{\mathbf k'}\hat b_{\mathbf k}\nonumber\\
            &+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}
            \sum_{\mathbf k,\mathbf q}\left[
                \alpha\hat d^\dagger_{\mathbf q}
                \hat b_{\mathbf q/2-\mathbf k}
                \hat b_{\mathbf q/2+\mathbf k}+\mathrm{H.c.}
            \right],
            \label{eq:appendix-ofr-hamiltonian}
        \end{align}
        where $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}=k^2/(2m)$.  The two collision paths are
        direct background scattering and optical conversion of an atom pair to
        the molecular state and back.  In the two-body sector, irreversible
        molecular decay is represented by
        \begin{equation}
            \widehat H_{\rm 2b}=\widehat H_{\rm OFR}
            -\frac{\ii\gamma}{2}\sum_{\mathbf q}
                \hat d^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\hat d_{\mathbf q}.
            \label{eq:appendix-nonhermitian-hamiltonian}
        \end{equation}
        This is the no-jump two-body Hamiltonian associated with the molecular
        Lindblad decay; the imaginary energy $-\ii\gamma/2$ is not an additional
        coherent light shift \cite{liu2024weakly}.

        Eliminating the molecule at relative collision energy $E$ produces the
        energy-dependent open-channel interaction
        \begin{equation}
            U_{\rm eff}(E)=g+
            \frac{|\alpha|^2}{E-\nu+\ii\gamma/2}.
            \label{eq:appendix-effective-interaction}
        \end{equation}
        The corresponding vacuum $T$ matrix is
        \begin{align}
            T(E)&=\left[U_{\rm eff}^{-1}(E)-\Pi(E)\right]^{-1},
            \label{eq:appendix-t-matrix}\\
            \Pi(E)&=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\mathbf k}
            \frac{1}{E-k^2/m+\ii0^+}.
            \label{eq:appendix-pair-propagator}
        \end{align}
        Because the contact model has no spatial range, $\Pi(E)$ is ultraviolet
        divergent.  Thus $g$, $\alpha$, and $\nu$ are bare quantities and
        cannot be inserted directly into a numerical prediction.

        Match Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-t-matrix} to the low-energy $s$-wave
        convention
        \begin{equation}
            T(E)=\frac{4\pi}{m}
            \frac{1}{\ac^{-1}(E)+\ii k},
            \qquad k=\sqrt{mE}.
            \label{eq:appendix-low-energy-t}
        \end{equation}
        With a momentum cutoff $\Lambda$, the zero-range renormalisation can be
        written
        \begin{align}
            \ac(E)&=\abg+
            \frac{m|\alpha_{\rm re}|^2}{4\pi}
            \frac{1}{E-\nu_{\rm re}+\ii\gamma/2},
            \label{eq:appendix-renormalised-ac}\\
            \frac{m}{4\pi\abg}&=\frac{1}{g}+\frac{m\Lambda}{2\pi^2},
            \label{eq:appendix-renormalised-background}\\
            |\alpha_{\rm re}|^2&=
            \frac{|\alpha|^2}{[1+mg\Lambda/(2\pi^2)]^2},
            \label{eq:appendix-renormalised-coupling}\\
            \nu_{\rm re}&=\nu-
            \frac{m\Lambda|\alpha|^2}{2\pi^2+mg\Lambda}.
            \label{eq:appendix-renormalised-detuning}
        \end{align}
        The cutoff dependence is thereby absorbed into the three observable
        low-energy parameters.  Defining the signed resonant strength
        \begin{equation}
            \Gamma_{\rm s}(I)=
            \frac{m|\alpha_{\rm re}(I)|^2}{4\pi\abg}
            \label{eq:appendix-signed-gamma}
        \end{equation}
        turns Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-renormalised-ac} into the dimensional
        one-pole map
        \begin{equation}
            \ac(E)=\abg\left[1+
            \frac{\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)}
            {E-\nu_{\rm re}(I)+\ii\gamma/2}\right].
            \label{eq:appendix-one-pole-map}
        \end{equation}
        Since $|\alpha_{\rm re}|^2\propto I$ in the weak-field regime,
        $|\Gamma_{\rm s}|\propto I$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:appendix-one-pole-map}
        follows from the reduced two-channel model.  The further identification
        \begin{equation}
            \nu_{\rm re}(I)\equiv\nu+\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)
            \label{eq:appendix-tied-shift}
        \end{equation}
        is a particular parameterisation of the renormalised molecular energy.
        It is not forced by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:appendix-renormalised-background}--
        \eqref{eq:appendix-renormalised-detuning}, nor is it a general physical
        identity between an optical linewidth and an AC Stark shift.

        Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-tied-shift} and then the threshold limit
        $E\rightarrow0$ yields the outline's formula,
        \begin{equation}
            \ac(I,\nu)=\abg\left[1+
            \frac{\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)}
            {-\nu-\Gamma_{\rm s}(I)+\ii\gamma/2}\right].
            \label{eq:appendix-outline-map}
        \end{equation}
        Thus the $-\Gamma_{\rm s}$ in the denominator records the tied-shift
        convention.  It should not be read as evidence that every physical OFR
        has a light shift equal to its resonant strength.

    \subsection{Loss sign and the implementation convention}

        The microscopic definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-signed-gamma}
        makes $\Gamma_{\rm s}$ have the same sign as $\abg$.  This point matters
        for $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$, where $\abg<0$ and therefore
        $\Gamma_{\rm s}<0$.  The code avoids a signed intensity bound by
        defining
        \begin{equation}
            s=\operatorname{sgn}(\abg),\qquad
            \Gamma=|\Gamma_{\rm s}|\geq0,qquad
            \Gamma_{\rm s}=s\Gamma.
            \label{eq:appendix-code-gamma}
        \end{equation}
        Substitution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-outline-map} gives exactly the
        implemented dimensional map, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensional}.  Hence the
        code constraint $0\leq\Gamma\leq\Gamma_{\max}$ is a bound on a
        magnitude; it is equivalent to
        $|\Gamma_{\rm s}|\leq\Gamma_{\max}$ in the microscopic convention.

        To expose the elastic and lossy parts, write
        $G=\Gamma_{\rm s}$, $\delta=\nu+G$, and $h=\gamma/2$.  Then
        \begin{align}
            \operatorname{Re}\ac
            &=\abg\left[1-
              \frac{G\delta}{\delta^2+h^2}\right],
            \label{eq:appendix-real-ac}\\
            \operatorname{Im}\ac
            &=-\abg\frac{Gh}{\delta^2+h^2}.
            \label{eq:appendix-imag-ac}
        \end{align}
        With $\ac=a-\ii b$ and $b\geq0$, one has
        \begin{equation}
            b=\abg\frac{G\gamma/2}
            {(\nu+G)^2+(\gamma/2)^2}\geq0,
            \label{eq:appendix-loss-length}
        \end{equation}
        because
        $\abg G=m|\alpha_{\rm re}|^2/(4\pi)>0$.  Equivalently, the code's
        $G=s\Gamma$ guarantees $\operatorname{Im}\ac\leq0$ for either sign of
        $\abg$.  Constraining a \emph{signed} $G\geq0$ while also using
        $\abg<0$ would instead violate this condition; that is not what the
        present implementation does.

        The dimensionless form follows with $u=\Gamma/\gamma$ and
        $v=\nu/\gamma$:
        \begin{align}
            \frac{\ac}{\abg}
            &=1+\frac{s u}{-v-s u+\ii/2},
            \label{eq:appendix-dimensionless-ac}\\
            \operatorname{Re}\frac{\ac}{\abg}
            &=1-\frac{s u(v+s u)}{(v+s u)^2+1/4},
            \label{eq:appendix-dimensionless-real}\\
            \operatorname{Im}\frac{\ac}{\abg}
            &=-\frac{s u/2}{(v+s u)^2+1/4}.
            \label{eq:appendix-dimensionless-imag}
        \end{align}
        For $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$, $s=-1$ and the resonant denominator is
        $u-v+\ii/2$, explaining why a model-internal resonance-tracking
        trajectory satisfies $v\simeq u$.

    \subsection{Limiting-case checks}

        These identities are useful independent checks on both the algebra and
        the implementation.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{No laser.} At fixed detuning,
            $\Gamma\rightarrow0$ gives $\ac\rightarrow\abg$.
            \item \textbf{Far from resonance.} For
            $|\nu+G|\gg\gamma$,
            \begin{equation}
                \ac\simeq\abg\left[1-\frac{G}{\nu+G}
                -\ii\frac{G\gamma}{2(\nu+G)^2}\right].
            \end{equation}
            If also $|\nu|\gg|G|$, the elastic correction falls as
            $1/\nu$ while loss falls as $1/\nu^2$.
            \item \textbf{No molecular decay.} For $\gamma=0$,
            \begin{equation}
                \ac=\abg\frac{\nu}{\nu+G}.
                \label{eq:appendix-lossless-limit}
            \end{equation}
            The zero is at $\nu=0$ and the pole at $\nu=-G$.  In particular,
            the fraction is $\nu/(\nu+G)$, not its reciprocal.
            \item \textbf{Finite molecular decay.} When $\gamma>0$, no real
            detuning produces a pole. The absorptive response is largest at
            $\delta=\nu+G=0$, while the pole lies off the real detuning axis.
        \end{itemize}

        not make its high-intensity or far-detuned extrapolation quantitative.

    \subsection{$^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ substitution and detuning origin}

        Once the reduced parameters are known, evaluating the map requires no
        separate van der Waals table.  A minimal input set is the signed
        background length, molecular decay rate, level-specific mapping from
        intensity to $\Gamma$, light-shift calibration, detuning origin, and one
        consistent frequency convention.  For the central
        $\abg=-1.4a_0$ value and the code's positive magnitude
        $\Gamma_f\geq0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ofr-dimensional} becomes
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\ac}{a_0}=-1.4\left[
            1-\frac{\Gamma_f}
            {\Gamma_f-\nu_f+\ii\gamma_f/2}\right],
            \label{eq:appendix-sr-explicit}
        \end{equation}
        where all subscript-$f$ quantities are ordinary frequencies.  Use
        $\gamma_f/2=7.5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for an ideal
        $15\,\mathrm{kHz}$ molecular line or
        $\gamma_f/2=20\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for the central experimentally fitted
        $40\,\mathrm{kHz}$ line.  The same equation may instead be evaluated in
        angular frequency after multiplying \emph{every} denominator term by
        $2\pi$.

        In the signed microscopic notation the shifted detuning is
        $\delta=\nu+\Gamma_{\rm s}$; in the code notation it is
        $\delta=\nu+s\Gamma$.  An experimental detuning may instead be quoted
        from the zero-intensity molecular line, the intensity-shifted line, the
        atomic $^1S_0\rightarrow{}^3P_1$ threshold, or a fitted photoassociation
        centre. These are not interchangeable.  The measured $0_u$ shift
        slopes for $n=-2,-3,-4$ are respectively about $-251(67)$,
        $+29(2)$, and $+4(1)\,\mathrm{kHz}/(\mathrm{W\,cm^{-2}})$
        \cite{blatt2011measurement}; their variation directly demonstrates why
        the tied shift in Eq.~\eqref{eq:appendix-tied-shift} must be tested rather
        than assumed during quantitative experimental translation.

        The practical conclusion is twofold.  The present map is a useful
        simplified theoretical control model: it has the correct qualitative
        background, dispersive, resonant-loss, and far-detuned structures and
        is well suited to automatic differentiation.  A mathematically optimal
        trajectory becomes a proposed $^{88}\mathrm{Sr}$ laser protocol only
        after the strength, light shift, linewidth, detuning origin, temperature
        range, and high-intensity response have all been calibrated for one
        selected molecular level.

\section{Superseded Note: Unknown Background Scattering Length and a Generic Reference Scale}
\label{app:temporary-generic-length-scale}
%==========================================================

    \textbf{Status of this note.} This section is a temporary explanation of the
    nondimensionalisation issue and should be reconciled with
    Sec.~\ref{sec:model} before the final report is submitted.  Its main point is
    that a reference length is a freely chosen positive unit, whereas the sign
    and magnitude of the background scattering length are physical inputs.  A
    change of units cannot determine or remove those physical inputs.

    \subsection{What can be chosen before the atomic species is known?}

        Start from the dimensional Volterra equation
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-dimensional-volterra}
            \left[
                \mathcal L_{1/2}^{(t)}
                +\frac{1}{4\pi a_s(t)}
            \right]\eta(t)=-1.
        \end{equation}
        Choose an arbitrary \emph{positive} reference length $L_0>0$ and define
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-generic-scales}
            t_0=\frac{mL_0^2}{\hbar},\qquad
            \tau=\frac{t}{t_0},\qquad
            \widetilde a_s=\frac{a_s}{L_0},\qquad
            \widetilde\eta=\frac{\eta}{L_0}.
        \end{equation}
        The factor $t_0=mL_0^2/\hbar$ makes the coefficient of the
        half-order operator dimensionless.  Equation~\eqref{eq:temporary-dimensional-volterra}
        then has the universal form
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-generic-volterra}
            \left[
                \widetilde{\mathcal L}_{1/2}^{(\tau)}
                +\frac{1}{4\pi\widetilde a_s(\tau)}
            \right]\widetilde\eta(\tau)=-1.
        \end{equation}
        Nothing in this step requires prior knowledge of $a_{\rm bg}$.  The
        choice of $L_0$ fixes the ruler and clock, but it does not specify the
        interaction measured with that ruler.

    \subsection{Where the unknown background scattering length enters}

        For the corrected optical map, define the resonant contribution
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-optical-factor}
            R(u,v)=\frac{u}{-v-u+\ii/2}.
        \end{equation}
        The stimulated coupling contains an inverse factor of
        $a_{\rm bg}$.  Its sign therefore cancels the background sign in the
        resonant correction, giving
        \begin{equation}
            a_s=a_{\rm bg}+|a_{\rm bg}|R(u,v).
        \end{equation}
        A generic nondimensionalisation contains both the signed background
        ratio and its positive magnitude,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-background-ratio}
            b=\frac{a_{\rm bg}}{L_0},
            \qquad r=\frac{|a_{\rm bg}|}{L_0}=|b|,
            \qquad \widetilde a_s=b+rR(u,v).
        \end{equation}
        Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporary-generic-volterra} gives
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-volterra-with-background}
            \left[
                \widetilde{\mathcal L}_{1/2}^{(\tau)}
                +\frac{1}{4\pi[b+rR(u,v)]}
            \right]\widetilde\eta(\tau)=-1.
        \end{equation}
        Thus $b$ is an input to the dynamics.  Its sign cannot be inferred by
        nondimensionalisation, and changing its sign is not a constant
        conversion applied after the calculation.  It changes the complex
        scattering-length history seen by the Volterra equation and can change
        both $\widetilde\eta$ and the optimal controls.  A genuinely
        species-independent calculation must consequently retain $b$ as a
        parameter or sweep over candidate positive and negative values.

        This is no different in principle from not knowing a mass, linewidth,
        or density before choosing an atom: the equations can be written with
        symbolic dimensionless parameters, but a species-specific numerical
        prediction requires those parameters to be supplied eventually.

    \subsection{The sign when using the background magnitude as reference}

        If one chooses
        \begin{equation}
            L_0=|a_{\rm bg}|,
            \qquad s=\operatorname{sgn}(a_{\rm bg}),
        \end{equation}
        then $r=1$, $b=s$, and the dimensionless scattering length is
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-signed-background}
            \widetilde a_s
            =\frac{a_s}{|a_{\rm bg}|}
            =s+\frac{u}{-v-u+\ii/2}.
        \end{equation}
        The dimensionless Volterra equation becomes
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-signed-ratio-volterra}
            \left[
                \widetilde{\mathcal L}_{1/2}^{(\tau)}
                +\frac{1}{4\pi[s+R(u,v)]}
            \right]\widetilde\eta(\tau)=-1.
        \end{equation}
        The sign therefore remains in the Volterra dynamics, but it does not
        reverse the lossy optical term.  Indeed,
        \begin{equation}
            \operatorname{Im}R(u,v)
            =-\frac{u/2}{(u+v)^2+1/4}\leq0
        \end{equation}
        for both values of $s$.  Hence
        $\operatorname{Im}(1/\widetilde a_s)\geq0$, so the implemented
        molecular objective remains non-negative for positive and negative
        background scattering lengths.  Changing $s$ can nevertheless change
        $\widetilde\eta$ and the optimal controls through the real background
        offset.

    \subsection{Does changing the reference length change the optimisation?}

        Merely changing $L_0$ does not change a fixed physical problem when
        every dimensionless quantity is transformed consistently.  For a
        physical duration $t_{\rm f}$, the solver horizon must be
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:temporary-duration-conversion}
            T=\frac{t_{\rm f}}{t_0}
             =\frac{\hbar t_{\rm f}}{mL_0^2}.
        \end{equation}
        At the same time, the solver must receive
        $b=a_{\rm bg}/L_0$ and the correspondingly scaled scattering length and
        pair amplitude.  Two calculations using different $L_0$ but describing
        the same $t_{\rm f}$, $a_{\rm bg}$, and laser history should then be
        related by a change of variables.

        By contrast, changing $L_0$ while retaining $T=1$ changes the physical
        duration to $t_{\rm f}=mL_0^2/\hbar$.  Likewise, replacing an unknown
        $b$ by $+1$ chooses a positive-background model.  These changes affect
        the optimisation itself and are not post-processing conversions.  In
        the present numerical implementation, $T$ enters the time step, the
        history kernel, the objective integral, and the smoothness penalty, so
        it cannot generally be altered only when plotting the final result.

    \subsection{A practical species-independent workflow}

        A clean workflow is:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Choose and document a positive reference length $L_0$.  It
            may be a fixed nominal length used across species or a physically
            motivated range scale.  It must not be a signed quantity.
            \item Keep $b=a_{\rm bg}/L_0$ as an explicit signed model
            parameter.  If the target species is not yet chosen, sample a
            physically motivated range of both signs rather than silently
            setting $b=1$.
            \item Specify or sweep the dimensionless horizon $T$.  Once an atom
            and a desired laboratory duration are selected, use
            Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporary-duration-conversion} to determine its
            appropriate value.
            \item Optimise using
            $\widetilde a_s=b+|b|R(u,v)$ in the Volterra recurrence and in the
            molecular objective.  With $L_0=|a_{\rm bg}|$, this reduces to
            $\widetilde a_s=s+R(u,v)$.  The recurrence algorithm does not need
            to be re-derived for each atom; only its dimensionless inputs
            change.
            \item After selecting a species, insert its mass, signed
            $a_{\rm bg}$, linewidth, density, and control calibration.  Check
            the zero-range and short-many-body-time validity window before
            interpreting the result as a laboratory protocol.
        \end{enumerate}

        In code, the scale choice $L_0=|a_{\rm bg}|$ is represented by the
        searchable scalar parameter \texttt{scattering\_sign}$=s$.  The
        implementation uses $s+R(u,v)$ and validates $s\in\{-1,+1\}$.

    \paragraph{Direct answer.}
        It was not incorrect to use the magnitude of the background scattering
        length as a convenient scale after a species had been selected.  It was
        incorrect to expect that choice to make an unknown signed
        $a_{\rm bg}$ universally equal to $+1$.  Before the species is known,
        either retain $b=a_{\rm bg}/L_0$ symbolically or include it in the
        parameter sweep.  After the species is known, choosing
        $L_0=|a_{\rm bg}|$ reduces $b$ to its sign.  That sign is added to the
        resonant term in the scattering map and must still be supplied to the
        $\eta$ calculation.

\section{Fixed Reference Time and Sweepable Background Ratio}
\label{app:fixed-reference-time}
%==========================================================

    A reference time $t_\star>0$ is fixed across candidate physical setups.
    It defines a positive length $\ell_\star=\sqrt{\hbar t_\star/m}$, not a
    removable physical scattering length. Consequently
    \begin{equation}
        r_{\rm bg}=\frac{a_{\rm bg}}{\ell_\star}
        =\sqrt{\frac{m}{\hbar t_\star}}\,a_{\rm bg}
    \end{equation}
    remains a signed model parameter. Sweeping $r_{\rm bg}$ represents
    candidate values of $a_{\rm bg}$ and $m$ at the fixed $t_\star$.

    With $s=\operatorname{sgn}(r_{\rm bg})$, the code evaluates
    \begin{equation}
        \widetilde a_s
        =r_{\rm bg}\left[1+\frac{s u}{-s u-v+\ii/2}\right]
        =|r_{\rm bg}|\left[s+\frac{u}{-s u-v+\ii/2}\right].
    \end{equation}
    The prefactor is $|r_{\rm bg}|$ because $r_{\rm bg}$ itself is signed;
    this guarantees the far-detuned or zero-width limit
    $\widetilde a_s=r_{\rm bg}$. The value $r_{\rm bg}=0$ is invalid because
    $s$ would be undefined, and there is no independent sign parameter in the
    configuration.

    Finally, the present regulariser is applied to first and second differences
    of the absolute dimensionless controls $u$ and $v$, with powers of the
    dimensionless time step shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:smoothness}.  It is therefore
    not invariant under a change of control cap or duration; this cap dependence
    is one reason the regularisation search is treated as part of the numerical
    methodology rather than as a calibrated hardware transfer function.
